AI LITERACY BOOTCAMP · KENYA

Understand AI.
Think critically.

A hands-on week for young people who want to know how AI really works, use it well, and question it when it gets things wrong.

Who it's forYoung people in Kenya, in or out of school
How long4 teaching days + 1 presentation day, 5 hours a day
What you needCuriosity. No AI knowledge, no coding

The programme

Your week, day by day

Day 1

Understanding Artificial Intelligence

Discover what artificial intelligence is, how it works and why it is becoming increasingly important in everyday life in Kenya. Featuring two live guest talks from the UK.

What you will learn

  • Understand the meaning of artificial intelligence and how it has evolved
  • The difference between artificial intelligence, machine learning and generative AI
  • How AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini and other intelligent assistants work
  • How AI generates responses and why it can sometimes produce incorrect information
  • The strengths, limitations and potential risks of AI
  • Examples of AI in everyday life: farming, health, mobile money, learning and jobs

2 guest talks on Teams
Day 2

How Machines Learn

Be the algorithm with 200 matatu trips from towns across Kenya, train your own image model in the browser, then try to break it.

What you will learn

  • The difference between traditional programming (rules in) and machine learning (examples in)
  • Data, features, labels, training and testing, explained with matatu-delay data
  • Find patterns in 200 matatu trips, write your own rules, and test them on 40 trips you have never seen
  • Train an image-recognition model with no code in Teachable Machine, and find out what makes it fail
  • Why accuracy can mislead, and why a missed illness is worse than a false alarm
  • How incomplete or unfair data becomes biased AI, with real case studies

DAY 2 READING

Gender Shades

Joy Buolamwini & Timnit Gebru, Conference on Fairness, Accountability and Transparency, 2018

Two researchers tested face-analysis systems from three big tech companies and found they worked almost perfectly on lighter-skinned men but failed far more often on darker-skinned women, because of who was missing from the training data. Read the introduction and the results, then come ready to discuss: who should have caught this, and how would you test an AI tool used in Kenya?

Day 3

Using AI Well

Prompt it, check it, own it. The practical skills you will use every week, in school, work and daily life.

What you will learn

  • Write better prompts with the R-T-C-F pattern: Role, Task, Context, Format
  • Use AI as a study partner (explain, quiz, plan), not a ghost-writer
  • Verify any claim in minutes with the SIFT method
  • Spot deepfakes and AI-powered scams, and why checking the source beats spotting glitches
  • What honest AI use looks like in school and at work, and how to write an AI-use disclosure

Day 4

Responsible AI and the Future

Who is responsible when AI gets it wrong? Privacy, high-stakes AI decisions, jobs, Africa's AI future, and a live debate.

What you will learn

  • What is safe to share with AI tools, and your rights under Kenya's Data Protection Act, 2019
  • Why big decisions about health, money and school need a human in the loop: automation complacency, black boxes and skill fade

  • Kenya's National AI Strategy 2025–2030 at a glance
  • Debate: should schools use facial recognition to take attendance? Then finish your Day 5 presentation

Day 5

Presentation Day: AI Under the Microscope

Today you show what you understand. Your group explains and judges a real AI system in front of a panel.

Your presentation must cover

  1. What the AI system is and who uses it
  2. How it works: data → learning → output
  3. A demo, including one check you made on it
  4. Benefits, backed by evidence
  5. Risks, limits and bias
  6. Three rules for responsible use
  7. How you used AI to prepare (disclosure)
  8. At least three credible sources

Format: 10 minutes + 5 minutes of questions, 6–10 slides. Every team member speaks, and the judges will ask about your sources.

Glossary

AI words in plain language

Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Technology that lets computers do tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as recognising faces, understanding speech, translating or making predictions.
Machine Learning (ML)
A type of AI that learns patterns from examples (data) instead of following rules written by a person.
Deep Learning
Machine learning using large neural networks. It powers face recognition, speech recognition and translation.
Generative AI
AI that creates new content (text, images, audio, video) instead of only sorting or predicting. ChatGPT, Gemini and image generators are examples.
Large Language Model (LLM)
The type of model behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. Trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word, again and again, which produces what feels like conversation.
Prompt
The instruction or question you give an AI tool. Better prompts include a role, a task, context and a format (R-T-C-F).
Hallucination
A confident AI answer that is false or invented, such as a fake reference or a made-up fact. Always verify important claims.
Data
The examples an AI learns from. A model only knows what its data has shown it: incomplete or unfair data leads to incomplete or unfair AI.
Feature
A clue the model uses to make a prediction. For a matatu trip: weather, time of day, whether it waited long to fill up.
Label
The answer the model is trying to predict, e.g. "delayed: yes or no". Labels are given during training.
Training and Testing
The model learns from training data, then is tested on data it has never seen. Testing on data it already saw is like seeing the exam paper early.
Supervised Learning
Learning from examples that come with the correct answers. The most common type of machine learning.
Unsupervised Learning
Learning without answers: the model finds groups or patterns in the data by itself.
Accuracy and Baseline
Accuracy is the percentage of correct predictions. Always compare it with a baseline, such as "always guess the most common answer", because high accuracy can hide a useless model.
False Alarm vs Miss
The two kinds of mistake. In high-stakes systems, a miss (such as an undetected illness) is usually far worse than a false alarm.
Bias in AI
When an AI system works worse for, or treats unfairly, certain groups, usually because its data under-represented them or reflected past unfairness.
Deepfake
AI-generated or AI-altered images, audio or video that make someone appear to say or do something they never did.
SIFT
Four moves to check a claim: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to the original.
Human in the Loop
A design where a qualified person reviews or makes the final decision. The class rule: the AI informs, a qualified human decides, and the decision is recorded.
Automation Complacency
When people trust a system that is usually right so much that they stop checking it, and miss the rare time it is wrong.

Feedback

How was your week?

Anonymous. No names, no pressure. This takes about three minutes and helps us make the next bootcamp even better.

What the form asks

  1. What are you doing right now: school, college, work, business, or looking for work? (Short answer)
  2. Before this week, how often did you use AI tools? (Never / Sometimes / Often)
  3. After this week, how confident do you feel explaining and using AI? (1 = not at all → 5 = very confident)
  4. Speak freely: what did you enjoy about the bootcamp?
  5. What would you suggest to make it better, and what did you not enjoy?

No name required. All responses go to the course team.
